CogAT explained

What is the CogAT?

The Cognitive Abilities Test — CogAT for short — measures reasoning and problem-solving abilities in three areas. It's not a test of what a child has memorized; it's designed to assess how they think. Many school districts use it as one criterion for gifted or highly capable program placement.

Test structure

Three batteries, nine subtests

The CogAT is divided into three batteries. Each battery contains three subtests, for nine subtests in total. Students may take all three batteries (the full battery) or a single battery, depending on how the district chooses to administer the test.

Verbal battery
Measures reasoning with words and language — analogies, classifications, and sentence completion. Students are asked to find relationships between words and complete patterns in language, not to recall facts.
Quantitative battery
Measures reasoning with numbers and mathematical relationships — number sequences, number puzzles, and equation building. It tests logical thinking with quantities, not arithmetic computation.
Nonverbal battery
Measures reasoning with shapes and figures — classifications, analogies, and paper folding. No reading or math is required; students work entirely with abstract visual patterns. This battery is sometimes described as the most language-independent of the three.
Test levels

CogAT levels and grades

The CogAT is offered at different levels that correspond roughly to grade level. Each level uses question formats and vocabulary appropriate for the intended age group. A student takes the level matched to their current grade — not their ability level. Level assignment is handled by the district or school, not by the family.

Scores

How CogAT scores work

CogAT results are reported as Standard Age Scores (SAS), Percentile Rank (PR), and a Stanine (S) — a 9-point scale where 9 is the highest. Scores are compared to other students of the same age, not the same grade. Most gifted programs look at Stanine 7 or above as a starting threshold, though the exact cutoff varies by district and program.

The scores are a snapshot of how a student performed on a particular day, not a fixed measure of potential. Familiarity with the question formats — understanding what each subtest is actually asking — makes a real difference in performance.
