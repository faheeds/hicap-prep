Highly capable programs

Gifted testing and HICAP admissions — how it works

Gifted or "highly capable" programs go by different names in different states — HICAP, Highly Capable, Talented and Gifted (TAG), GATE, and others. The admissions process typically involves a referral, a cognitive abilities test (often the CogAT), and a review by a district committee. This page explains what to expect and how to support your child through the process.

The program

What is a highly capable program?

Highly capable (HICAP) programs serve students who demonstrate advanced cognitive reasoning abilities that set them meaningfully apart from their age peers. These programs are typically separate from grade-level classes and offer a more demanding curriculum, faster pacing, and a peer environment of students with similar abilities.

The term "HICAP" is common in Washington state; other states use similar programs under different names. The details — how many seats exist, how scores are weighted, whether sibling preference applies — vary by district. Always verify the specifics with your school district directly.

The typical process

How the referral and testing process works

Referral
A student is typically referred by a parent, teacher, or school counselor. Some districts have universal screening — every student in a certain grade is assessed — while others require an explicit referral form. Check with your school about the process and the deadline for submitting a referral.
Cognitive abilities testing
Referred students take a cognitive abilities test, most commonly the CogAT. The test is group-administered at the school, typically during a regular school day. No special materials are needed; students sit the test alongside their classmates who were also referred.
Portfolio or teacher review
Many districts supplement the test score with a portfolio review, teacher ratings, or an achievement test to build a fuller picture of the student. A high CogAT score alone is often not sufficient; districts look at multiple data points.
Committee decision
A district-level committee reviews the combined data and determines placement. If your child does not qualify in a given year, most districts allow re-referral in subsequent years — and familiarity with the test format genuinely helps on the second attempt.
Preparing your child

What actually helps with CogAT preparation

The CogAT measures reasoning, not memorized content — so traditional studying doesn't apply. What does help is familiarity with how each subtest works. A student who has never seen a paper-folding question before will spend mental energy figuring out what's being asked, while a student who has worked through a few dozen will spend that energy on the actual reasoning.

This is what HiCap Prep is designed for: repeated exposure to all three batteries' question formats, with a structured 10-week plan, parent-run timed mock tests, and questions that never repeat within a session so the practice stays cognitively active rather than rote.
